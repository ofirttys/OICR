Fertility Preservation in Adolescents with Cancer

A decision aid to help you and your family understand and compare your two main fertility preservation options: Egg Freezing and Ovarian Tissue Freezing.

Who is this for? This decision aid is for post-pubertal adolescents with ovaries who are about to begin gonadotoxic (cancer-related) treatment, and their parents or guardians. It is meant to be used together with your fertility specialist's advice — not instead of it.

How Cancer Treatment Affects Fertility

Your ovaries and your eggs

You are born with all the eggs you will ever have — about 1 million at birth, declining to roughly 300,000 by puberty. Eggs exist at different stages of development inside your ovaries:

  • Primordial follicles — the vast majority; tiny, dormant, and protected deep in the ovary
  • Growing follicles — gradually maturing over 90–150 days
  • Antral follicles — the small group visible on ultrasound each month
  • Mature (MII) eggs — the only eggs that can be fertilized; ~1 per month naturally

What chemotherapy does

Many chemotherapy drugs, particularly cyclophosphamide and the alkylating agents used in stem cell transplant preparation, damage the ovarian follicle pool. This can cause:

  • Reduced egg supply (reduced ovarian reserve)
  • Premature menopause (ovaries stop working before age 40)
  • Difficulty or inability to become pregnant naturally
  • Loss of hormone production, affecting puberty and bone health

The extent of damage depends on the type and dose of chemotherapy, and your age.

Key point: OTC preserves a much larger and earlier follicle pool. OC captures only the small subset of eggs that are already mature.

⚠️ Important note about egg quality in adolescents Research shows that aneuploidy (abnormal chromosome number) in eggs follows a U-shaped curve — it is elevated in adolescents, reaches a low point in young adulthood (ages 20–32), then rises again with age. This means adolescent eggs may have a higher rate of chromosomal errors than adult eggs, which can affect the chances of a successful pregnancy using frozen eggs.

Your Two Options: Side-by-Side

Both egg freezing and ovarian tissue freezing are real options for post-pubertal adolescents. Neither is right for everyone — the best choice depends on your medical situation, your values, and your goals. This table is a starting point for that conversation.

Topic 🔵 Egg Freezing (OC)
Oocyte Cryopreservation		 🟢 Ovarian Tissue Freezing (OTC)
Ovarian Tissue Cryopreservation
What It Involves
Procedure Hormone injections daily for ~10–14 days to stimulate egg growth, then egg retrieval under sedation using a needle through the vagina or abdomen A short laparoscopic (keyhole) surgery under general anesthesia to remove all or part of one ovary; tissue is frozen in strips
What is preserved Mature eggs (MII oocytes) — ready to be fertilized in the future Ovarian cortex tissue containing thousands of primordial (dormant) follicles — the bulk of your egg supply
How eggs are used later Eggs are thawed, fertilized with sperm (IVF/ICSI), and transferred into the uterus as embryos Tissue is thawed and transplanted back into the body; eggs can then mature naturally, leading to spontaneous pregnancy or IVF
Timing & Urgency
Time before cancer treatment can start ⏱ Requires 2–3 weeks delay for ovarian stimulation. "Random start" protocols exist but add complexity. ✅ Can be done within days — as soon as an operating room is available. No delay to cancer therapy (chemo can start within 24–48 hours).
Can it be done after chemotherapy starts? ✗ No — chemotherapy damages eggs in a way that makes OC inadvisable once treatment has begun ✓ Yes — tissue can be harvested after some chemotherapy in selected situations, especially when a patient initially couldn't have the procedure
Accessibility & Geography
Where can it be done? Requires proximity to a fertility clinic with an embryology lab. Monitoring visits must be in-person, near the clinic. Surgery can be performed at a local hospital; tissue is transported to a specialized cryopreservation centre (hub-and-spoke model)
Travel required? ⚠️ Significant — patient must stay near the fertility clinic for 2–3+ weeks Minimal — patient stays home; only the tissue travels to the specialized centre ("The patient stays — the tissue moves")
Procedural Considerations for Adolescents
Physical demands Daily self-injections, frequent blood draws and ultrasounds, vaginal exam or abdominal monitoring, sedation for retrieval. May be distressing for adolescents who have not been sexually active. General anesthesia (one-time), laparoscopic surgery. Major complication rate reported as under 1–3.4% in pediatric/adolescent cohorts. Can be combined with other procedures under the same anesthetic.
Cultural / religious considerations Vaginal access may be a concern in some cultural or religious contexts. Transabdominal or transrectal alternatives have been described in adults but are not well-studied in adolescents. No vaginal procedure required. Surgery is performed abdominally.
Risk of ovarian hyperstimulation ⚠️ Yes — adolescents with high ovarian reserve are at increased risk. Mild in ~10%, severe in <1%. Not applicable
Biological Considerations
Egg quality concerns ⚠️ Important — adolescent eggs have elevated aneuploidy (chromosome errors). More eggs may be needed to achieve a live birth compared to adults. Primordial follicles are considered more resilient to cryoinjury. Long-term genetic effects of cryopreservation are still under study.
Restores natural hormone function? ✗ No — OC does not address hormone production. Does not prevent premature menopause. ✓ Yes — in over 90% of cases, ovarian tissue transplantation restores hormone function and menstrual cycles within 4–5 months
Risk of reintroducing cancer cells Not applicable ⚠️ Theoretical risk for patients with blood cancers (leukemia) or cancers that spread to the ovaries. Tissue is assessed before transplantation. Recent data support safety in leukemia patients who were in remission at time of harvest.
Future Use
Future procedure to use preserved material Egg thawing → ICSI fertilization → embryo transfer. Requires IVF clinic. Partner or donor sperm needed at time of use. Tissue thawing → laparoscopic transplantation → natural or assisted conception. Spontaneous pregnancy is possible after transplantation.
Chance of spontaneous pregnancy? ✗ No — IVF is always required ✓ Yes — spontaneous pregnancies have occurred after tissue transplantation

What Do We Know About Outcomes?

⚠️ Important limitation Most of the outcome data below come from adult patients. Data specific to adolescents are very limited. This means it is harder to predict your individual chances of future pregnancy. Your fertility specialist can discuss what these numbers mean for your specific situation.

🔵 Egg Freezing (OC)

~90% of frozen eggs survive the thaw process (adult data)
70–75% of thawed eggs fertilize successfully (adult data)
39–47% live birth rate per transfer with 4–8 donor eggs (young donors — closest approximation to adolescent patients)
~14 median eggs retrieved in adolescents aged 13–18 (Latif et al., 2025)
⚠️ Only 1 live birth in an adolescent (<18 years) has been reported from OC in the published literature (as of systematic review by Slonim et al., 2023). The optimal number of eggs to freeze for adolescents to achieve a live birth is unknown.

🟢 Ovarian Tissue Freezing (OTC)

>90% of patients recover ovarian hormonal function after tissue transplantation
~32% live birth rate after OTC and transplantation (systematic review and meta-analysis, Ní Dhonnabháin et al., 2022)
9 years old youngest age at tissue cryopreservation where live birth has been reported after transplantation
✓ Spontaneous pregnancies have been reported after transplantation in adolescent and adult patients
⚠️ Long-term reproductive outcome data in patients who cryopreserved tissue as adolescents remain limited. The transplantation step is a future surgery that not all patients may proceed with.

I am medically fragile or need to start treatment urgently
OTC is feasible in urgent situations and in medically unstable patients; OC may not be

Glossary of Terms

Aneuploidy
An abnormal number of chromosomes in an egg or embryo, which can prevent a healthy pregnancy. More common in adolescent and older adult eggs.
Antral follicle
A fluid-filled follicle visible on ultrasound, containing a maturing egg. These are the ones targeted during egg freezing stimulation.
Cryopreservation
Freezing biological material (eggs, tissue) at very low temperatures to preserve it for future use.
Gonadotoxic
Harmful to the reproductive organs (ovaries or testes). Many chemotherapy drugs are gonadotoxic.
Hub-and-spoke model
A system where ovarian tissue is collected at local hospitals (spokes) and transported to a specialized cryopreservation centre (hub), so patients don't need to travel.
ICSI
Intracytoplasmic Sperm Injection — a type of IVF where a single sperm is injected directly into an egg. Recommended for frozen-thawed eggs.
IVF
In Vitro Fertilization — eggs are fertilized with sperm in a lab to create embryos, which are then transferred into a uterus.
Laparoscopy
A minimally invasive (keyhole) surgical technique using small incisions and a camera. Used for ovarian tissue harvest in OTC.
Oocyte
An egg cell. A mature oocyte (MII) is the kind that can be fertilized by sperm.
Ovarian cortex
The outer layer of the ovary, which contains the primordial follicle pool. This is the tissue that is harvested and frozen in OTC.
Ovarian reserve
The quantity and quality of eggs remaining in your ovaries. Measured by AMH blood test and antral follicle count on ultrasound.
Premature ovarian insufficiency (POI)
When the ovaries stop working properly before age 40, causing low estrogen, irregular periods, and reduced fertility.
Primordial follicle
The smallest, dormant follicle — the most abundant type. OTC preserves these. They can remain dormant for years before activating.
Random-start stimulation
An OC protocol that can be started at any point in the menstrual cycle (not just the start), to shorten the delay before cancer treatment.
